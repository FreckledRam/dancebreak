01

It started at a party.

Competition was part of breaking from its first night.

  1. 1970s

    The Bronx, New York City

    Black and Latin American teens host parties at home and in public parks. The energy of funk and soul pushes some dancers toward more dynamic movement. They borrow from gymnastics and martial arts, then invent moves that belong to nothing else. The drive is to stand out and be seen as the coolest in the room.

  2. Crews

    An alternative to the street

    Breaking grows up alongside the art and music of hip hop, offering young people in under-resourced neighborhoods something other than gang activity and violence. Dancers form crews and battle rival crews at public events and nightclubs.

  3. Early 1980s

    The world notices

    Breakers appear on television and in movies. Crews are flown across the country and around the world to show the new phenomenon, and the people watching take up the dance too.

  4. Late 1980s

    The drop

    Commercialization moves faster than it can last, and public interest falls sharply. Rap music keeps its place in the mainstream. Hip hop dance moves on from breaking.

  5. After

    Carrying the torch

    The most passionate breakers keep organizing events, teaching the dance and motivating the next generation. Over time the informal battles become official competitions, with trophies and prize money.

  6. 2024

    Paris

    Breaking is contested at the Summer Olympic Games, with a governing body and a standard judging system for the first time.

02

Four kinds of movement.

Each holds more individual moves than anyone could list, and every breaker has invented their own.

Toprock

Movements performed while standing.

Footwork

Movements performed down on the ground, on the hands and feet.

Powermoves

Dynamic movements involving spinning and flipping.

Freezes

Static held poses.

03

How a competition runs.

One on one, or crew against crew, in front of a panel of 3, 5, 7 or 9 judges who are usually experienced breakers themselves.

Stage one

The showcase

Every entrant performs alone. Each judge scores each showcase, typically from 0 to 10 or 0 to 100. The top scores go through.

Top 8 advance

Stage two

The bracket

Single elimination. Two competitors take turns for a set number of rounds, then the judges pick a winner. Lose once and you are out.

Until one is left

No league.
No rulebook.

Basketball has FIBA. Tennis has the ITF. Gymnastics has the FIG. Breaking has no single authority deciding who can enter, what scores, or how a battle is won. It is a patchwork of events, from local grassroots jams to shows run by multinational corporations, and every event can set its own rules.

To reach the Olympic Games it needed a governing body and one set of rules. The World DanceSport Federation, the governing body of competitive ballroom dance, took that role. With no history in the breaking community, its authority has stayed mostly within its own events.

04

You be the judge.

The traditional way: when the battle ends, each judge points left or right, or crosses their arms for a tie. Most votes wins. It is simple, and it is criticized for being not consistent, not transparent and not measurable. So other systems were tried. Pick one and take seat 3.

05

Where the scores come from.

Results live on the platforms that ran the judging. This site reads them once a day and joins them into one dataset. It used to be a slow job done by hand.

And8Builds and runs the judging technology, so every event that uses it lands here. WDSFHolds the results of all of the federation's own events. Break KonnectAn event platform used mostly by smaller, local grassroots events. Original collectionThe hand-built dataset this site was seeded from.

Breaker rankings

Elo rating from every recorded 1 vs 1 battle. A breaker is ranked after 5 battles. Click a breaker for their history.

People are matched by name. Two breakers who share a name are counted as one; one breaker with two names is counted as two. Coverage before 2025 is incomplete, which affects ratings.

Judges

How each judge votes compared with the rest of the panel. Click a judge for the systems they have judged.

With majority: share of a judge's round votes that matched the panel's majority. Lone dissent: share where they were the only judge on their side. Votes for red: 50% would be even.

Judging systems

How each system behaves in practice across the battles that used it.

Judges and Rounds are averages per battle. Unanimous: rounds where every judge picked the same side. One-vote: rounds decided by a single judge. Red wins: share of decided battles won by the red side.
